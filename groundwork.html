A reading

What a building owes its ground.

There is a phrase that has become difficult to use precisely. Anchored in culture — written into briefs, repeated in design statements, illustrated by surface treatments that vary from one geography to another but rarely change what a building is for. Used this way, the phrase describes a stylistic register : a vocabulary, a palette, an iconography drawn from a place and applied to a programme that could just as easily have come from anywhere.

There is another way to take the phrase, which produces different architecture and, more importantly, a different kind of long-term asset. Taken structurally, anchored in culture is not a set of forms to deploy but a question to keep open during the early decisions of a project : what does this ground — its climate, its history, its way of gathering people, its way of producing food or shelter — oblige us to reconsider in the programme itself, in the order of decisions, in what we will not do?

The two readings do not yield the same buildings. They do not yield the same returns either. The first absorbs cultural references at the level of the envelope ; the second lets them work in the conception, where they shape what gets built and what does not.

The three projects that follow document how this question has been kept open, in three contexts that posed it differently. They are not arranged as variations on a theme. They trace a movement — from a context where the ground situates a programme, to a context where it structures it, to a context where it organises the very grammar of the building itself.

First movement · Régénération

Cité Internationale de la Gastronomie et du Vin — where the ground situates a programme.

Hectares of dormant heritage land

Six and a half hectares of dormant heritage land in the centre of Dijon, transformed into a mixed programme : a hotel school (Ferrandi), two Pathé multiplexes with nine screens, a four-star Hilton with 125 rooms, shops, restaurants, a wine merchant, an entrepreneurial hub with offices, and an eco-district extending the operation to roughly 85,000 m².

The temptation, on a site of this size, is to treat the cultural identity of the place — Burgundian gastronomy and viticulture — as a marketing surface : a name, a façade material, a choice of colours. The Cité is not built that way. Gastronomy is the organising principle of the programme itself.

The hotel school is not a cultural sign placed inside the building ; it forms the people who will run the place. The shops are not a leasing strategy presented as cultural anchorage ; they stage the regional production economy that gives the operation its reason for being there rather than somewhere else. The mixed programme is not a financial diversification arranged after the fact ; it is the spatial translation of an ecosystem that already existed in the region and that the project has gathered into one place.

A district built on a stylistic reference can be replicated ; a district built around a regional economy cannot. The latter holds its value because its programme cannot be detached from its ground.
Culture as structure — not as decoration
Hinge

When the ground does more than situate.

In Dijon, the ground situates. It gives the programme its economic logic and its long-term reason to be where it is. In other contexts, the ground does more than this.

Climate, prevailing winds, available materials, ways of gathering shade and managing scarcity stop being technical parameters consulted late in a project ; they become constraints of conception, as structuring as the programme itself. The further one moves from temperate Europe, the less the distinction holds between the brief and what the brief had to take into account from the beginning.

The second project documents what changes when the ground starts asking questions that cannot be answered after the building has been drawn.

Second movement · Climate as structure

A.S.A.R.I. — where the ground structures the programme.

Location
Foum el Oued, Morocco — where the Sahara meets the Atlantic
Programme
African Sustainable Agriculture Research Institute
Commissioned by
Phosboucraa Foundation · OCP
Note
First stone of the future Mohamed VI Polytechnic University of Laâyoune
Status
Inaugurated early 2022 · Off-grid
80%
Energy passivity target

The project was not conceived as a building first and a sustainability strategy later. It was conceived as a reading of its site. Before any architectural choice, the orientation of the ensemble was decided in response to the dominant wind from the north-northeast, the shifting of sand, the position of the sea — with a target of 80% energy passivity built into the conception itself.

A covered promenade in steel cables and a sun-shading fabric — its colour drawn from the Sahrawi tradition — connects the research units along a single spine. The colour is not a sign placed on the building ; it is a structural protection from solar radiation that simultaneously anchors the ensemble in its cultural ground. The two functions cannot be separated.

The same logic governs the architecture itself. The campus is conceived as an avant-garde eco-responsible facility targeting 80% energy passivity. Laboratories are given a four-metre ceiling height to ensure excellent natural lighting ; interior patios use the prevailing winds to ventilate workspaces and laboratories naturally. The exterior façades are dimensioned through parametric design, calculated to respond to the precise solar exposure of each lit surface — a method that draws on the contemporary tools of computation but produces an architecture that the inhabitants of this coast would recognise.

"Inspired by the knowledge of the ancients, nourished by our parametric design tools" : the formula is not rhetorical. It describes the actual sequence of decisions.

Hinge

When the refusal of imported models becomes the model itself.

A.S.A.R.I. shows that climate can structure a programme — that the elements of a place can shape the building rather than be shaped by it.

The next step in the same line of thought asks something more demanding : can the social culture of a place, its way of gathering, transmitting, and learning, become the organising principle of a building, and not only its decorative frame? The distinction matters. Architecture that draws from a local heritage produces references that can be admired. Architecture that lets that heritage organise its very grammar produces buildings that could not have been designed in the absence of that heritage. The third project moves to that point.

Third movement · Architecture as pedagogy

ISM Dakar — where the ground organises the very grammar of the building.

Location
Dakar, Senegal — latitude 14.7° north
Programme
Institut Supérieur de Management — campus for 3,000 students
Constraint
Constrained urban site · intense year-round solar gain
Distinction
First vertical campus in West Africa
Photography
© Fabio Mantovani
60%
Reduction in annual direct solar gain

A new campus for the Institut Supérieur de Management in Dakar, designed to receive three thousand students. The project does not begin with form. It begins with a figure : the arbre à palabres, the palaver tree — the place under which conversation circulates freely, where village life is discussed and where elders pass knowledge to children. The school before the school.

This is not invoked as a metaphor or as the name of an atrium ; it organises the principal façade and the disposition of every space on the campus. The whole reads as an educational village structured around a central reference point, the way a settlement organises itself around the tree that gives it its civic centre.

Verticality, here, is not a stylistic option. The site is too small to accommodate three thousand students horizontally. The architectural response was to ask whether the constraint could be turned into a pedagogical opportunity rather than a spatial compromise. Each level of the building extends outdoor terraces — open spaces conceived as suspended versions of the original tree, places of pause, study, and informal exchange. The building's vertical organisation is therefore not an imitation of glass towers found elsewhere ; it is the consequence of the same logic that placed the palaver tree at the centre, applied to a site that could not accept it on the ground.

The performance follows from the grammar. The southern façade is sculpted volumetrically to create deep shaded recesses ; an exoskeleton of vertical terracotta brise-soleil protects the east, south and west elevations ; a shading sail covers eighty-nine per cent of the roof. The result is a documented reduction of 60 per cent in annual direct solar gain compared to an unprotected volume of the same constructibility. The common spaces operate without mechanical cooling. None of these decisions can be detached from the figure that organises the campus.

This campus does not replicate models from elsewhere. It follows a deeply contextual approach, designed in Dakar, for Dakar, and with Africa.
The sentence reads as a manifesto. It describes an operational reality.
Adjacent practice

Five further projects working the same line.

Closing reading

What three projects, eight readings, leave us with.

Working on land at this scale changes what an architectural decision is. On a single building, an early choice can often be revised later, at a cost that the project absorbs. On a programme that runs across millions of square metres, decisions taken in the first weeks govern what is possible in the next twenty years.

Cultural anchorage, sustainability targets, programmatic mix, the relation of the building to its ground — none of these can be added at a later stage. They are either present in the early conception, or they are absent from the asset.

What these three projects have in common is not a vocabulary, nor a sustainability standard, nor a regional reference. It is the moment at which the questions were asked. The brief was never executed before being interrogated. The cultural reading was never validated before being tested against the climate, the social grammar, the long-term economy. The sequence holds across geographies that share almost nothing else.

A practice that holds this sequence as a discipline is recognisable to one thing : it brings a method to the room before it brings images. The conversation that opens here does not need to begin with renderings or precedents. It begins with the questions that, once answered well, govern what the renderings will eventually have to show.

The practice

For more than a hundred years, the Bechu family has built places that last. Today in its fourth generation, the practice brings together urbanists, architects, heritage architects and designers around a shared conviction : architecture must hold complexity — living programmes, inhabited estates, demanding climates, long-horizon investment decisions — without diluting it.

Working with landowners and investors at the moment they shift from land aggregation to direct development is a particular registration of the practice. It calls for an architectural partner who can read the terrain before drawing on it, structure a development sequence rather than execute single buildings, and integrate cultural and climatic anchorage in the early decisions of a programme rather than at the level of its surface treatments. This is the line of work the three projects above document — and the line through which the practice approaches development at the scale of millions of square metres in environments where the ground asks more of architecture than situation.

This tradition extends across registers : housing, tertiary, hospitality, urban regeneration, cultural facilities, heritage monuments. It is supported by a dedicated practice in interior architecture, Volume ABC, which intervenes on the programmes — hospitality, high-end residential, mixed-use commercial bases — where the architecture of the building and the architecture of the experience inside it cannot be separated. Across all these registers, one constant : the discipline of a shared protocol as the condition of the result.

"For a project to last, it must come from its ground — and each of our decisions is guided by the responsibility we hold toward the living." Clémence Bechu — Managing Director
